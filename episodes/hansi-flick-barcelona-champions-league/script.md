### Scene 1 — HOOK
**Type**: portrait (Flick, dynamic touchline pose, arms crossed, intense focus)
**Est. duration**: ~35s

> Barcelona have just won back-to-back league titles under Hansi Flick. They have more attacking talent on that pitch than almost anyone else in Europe. And for the second year running, their season ended the same way: not with the trophy that actually matters to them, but with a Champions League exit that exposed the same weakness both times. So this is the real question going into this season: is Hansi Flick actually the coach who fixes that? Or is he the coach who's shown us, twice now, exactly where the ceiling is?

### Scene 2 — Context: the Bayern proof of concept
**Type**: portrait (younger Flick, Bayern touchline, 2020 era)
**Est. duration**: ~30s

> Start with what we know for certain: Flick can win the Champions League. In November 2019, he took over Bayern Munich as an emergency appointment, an assistant thrown into the job mid-season. Six months later, Bayern had won everything there was to win.

### Scene 3 — Data-graphic: the 2020 sextuple
**Type**: chalkboard/data-graphic (trophy stack building up, six icons)
**Est. duration**: ~20s

> The Bundesliga. The German Cup. The Champions League, beaten in the final without losing a single game that campaign. Then the UEFA Super Cup, the German Super Cup, and the Club World Cup followed in the months after. Six trophies. This wasn't a coach getting lucky once — this was total, generational domination.

### Scene 4 — Context: the exit from Bayern
**Type**: portrait (Flick, tense boardroom-adjacent framing)
**Est. duration**: ~25s

> And yet, a year later, he was gone — a breakdown in his relationship with Bayern's sporting director over transfer strategy left him walking away from the best job in German football, at the peak of his powers.

### Scene 5 — Context: Germany, the contradiction
**Type**: data-graphic (results timeline sliding downward, national team badge)
**Est. duration**: ~35s

> What happened next is the part that should make everyone cautious about assuming Flick simply solves problems by showing up. He took the Germany national team job in 2021. It did not go well. A group-stage exit at the 2022 World Cup. Poor form through 2023. And in September of that year, after a heavy defeat to Japan, Flick was sacked — the first head coach in the history of the German national team to be dismissed mid-tenure. The same coach. A completely different outcome.

### Scene 6 — Context: Barcelona appointment
**Type**: portrait (Flick arriving at Camp Nou / training ground, July 2024)
**Est. duration**: ~30s

> So when Barcelona hired him in July 2024 to replace Xavi, they weren't just betting on the Bayern version of Hansi Flick. They were betting he'd learned something from the Germany version too.

### Scene 7 — Data-graphic: Season 1 domestic sweep
**Type**: chalkboard/data-graphic (La Liga, Copa del Rey, Supercopa icons stacking)
**Est. duration**: ~25s

> On the domestic front, it's hard to argue with the results. In his very first season, Flick won the treble at home: La Liga, the Copa del Rey, and the Spanish Super Cup. Barcelona looked, week to week, like the most dangerous attacking team in Europe.

### Scene 8 — Build-up: the Inter semi-final
**Type**: portrait (Flick and squad, tunnel, tension before the second leg)
**Est. duration**: ~30s

> Then came the Champions League semi-final against Inter Milan. And this tie is where the story of Flick's Barcelona really starts to take shape — because for ninety minutes across two legs, this looked like the night it all came together.

### Scene 9 — The collapse
**Type**: portrait (dramatic, San Siro, extra time, close on Flick's reaction)
**Est. duration**: ~50s

> Away at the San Siro, needing something special, Barcelona fell two goals behind by half-time. They didn't fold. They came storming back, and by the 88th minute, they were ahead, 3 to 2 on the night, on the verge of a final. Then it slipped away in the cruelest possible way. Francesco Acerbi equalized in the third minute of stoppage time. Extra time couldn't save them either — Davide Frattesi scored the winner in the 99th minute. Final score: 4 to 3. Barcelona were out, 7 to 6 on aggregate, in one of the most dramatic semi-finals the competition has ever produced.

### Scene 10 — Data-graphic: Season 2, same pattern
**Type**: data-graphic (La Liga trophy retained, red flag on Champions League line)
**Est. duration**: ~35s

> It would be easy to write that off as one chaotic night. Except the 2025-26 season told almost the same story with different details. Barcelona retained the La Liga title, finishing eight points clear of Real Madrid — genuinely dominant domestically for a second straight year. But in Europe, the same defensive fragility that cost them against Inter kept resurfacing, and Barcelona fell short again.

### Scene 11 — Chalkboard: naming the actual problem
**Type**: chalkboard tactical diagram (open spaces behind a high defensive line, red markers)
**Est. duration**: ~45s

> So what's actually going on here? Twice now, under two different sets of circumstances, Barcelona have looked completely in control domestically and structurally exposed in Europe specifically. The pattern both times points to the same root cause: a high-risk, high-line approach that manufactures huge attacking overloads against weaker Spanish sides, but leaves gaps in behind against the kind of elite, patient, ruthless opponents the Champions League's later rounds are built from. Inter didn't out-play Barcelona for ninety minutes. They waited for exactly those gaps, and they punished them when it mattered most.

### Scene 12 — Portrait: the summer 2026 response
**Type**: portrait (Flick in a presentation/press setting, summer 2026)
**Est. duration**: ~30s

> Barcelona's own hierarchy seems to agree with that diagnosis. This past summer, the club made it clear that going deep in Europe — not just winning Spain again — was the actual priority behind their transfer business.

### Scene 13 — Chalkboard: what the signings are meant to fix
**Type**: chalkboard/data-graphic (three player silhouettes with labeled tactical roles)
**Est. duration**: ~55s

> Three signings tell you what they think the fix looks like. Rodri arrives as exactly the kind of tempo-controlling midfield presence Barcelona have lacked — a player whose entire game is built around not conceding the exact transitions that beat them against Inter. Anthony Gordon and Karim Adeyemi both add pace and directness in wide areas, attackers who can finish games early rather than leaving them stretched out until the final third of the Champions League bites back.

### Scene 14 — Chalkboard: the open question
**Type**: chalkboard tactical diagram (before/after shape comparison)
**Est. duration**: ~40s

> On paper, that's a squad built to solve last year's specific failure. But new signings changing a shape on a whiteboard is very different from a manager actually being willing to sacrifice attacking risk for defensive control — and that's the tension that's followed Flick his entire career. Bayern in 2020 was daring and still won everything. Germany was daring and it got him sacked. Which version of that instinct shows up in Barcelona's biggest European nights this time is still an open question.

### Scene 15 — CONCLUSION
**Type**: portrait (Flick, calm, forward-looking touchline shot)
**Est. duration**: ~35s

> Hansi Flick has already proven he can win the biggest prize in club football. He's also proven, with a different team, that the same approach can completely fall apart. Barcelona now have two league titles, one devastating near-miss, and a rebuilt squad aimed directly at their one recurring weakness. Whether that's enough to finally get Flick back to a European final — or whether Barcelona are simply building toward another dramatic, three-goal collapse in the wrong place — is exactly what this season is going to answer.
