**Runtime target:** ~24–26 minutes
**Language:** English (narration), Arabic subtitles to be generated separately from this final text
**Voice:** single fixed channel voice (per style_guide.md)
**Visual system:** ILLUSTRATED PORTRAIT scenes (hand-drawn character art, Tifo-style) for narrative/character beats, DATA-GRAPHIC scenes (animated maps, timelines, stat charts, Dorktown-style) for numbers, transfers, tournament progress and results.

## CHAPTER 1 — THE BOY FROM CALDOGNO

**Scene 4 — PORTRAIT — 1:05–1:45 (40s)**
*Visual: Illustrated countryside town, Caldogno, northern Italy, 1970s. A boy juggling a ball alone against a wall.*

> Roberto Baggio was born on February 18th, 1967, in Caldogno, a small town near Vicenza in northern Italy — one of eight children in a working-class family.
> He wasn't the biggest kid on the pitch, or the fastest. What he had was a first touch that made grown coaches stop mid-sentence, and a left foot that seemed to know where the goal was before he did.
> By thirteen, he'd already signed with his hometown club, Vicenza. By fifteen, he was training with the first team.

**Scene 5 — DATA-GRAPHIC — 1:45–2:10 (25s)**
*Visual: Simple animated map — Caldogno → Vicenza → a growing scouting network of Serie A clubs sending representatives, illustrated as converging lines.*

> Word travels fast in Italian football. By his late teens, Baggio wasn't just a promising kid from the Veneto anymore — he was being watched by the biggest clubs in the country.
> In May 1985, Fiorentina agreed to sign him from Vicenza. He was eighteen years old, and it looked like the beginning of everything.
> It nearly wasn't the beginning of anything at all.

## CHAPTER 2 — THE INJURY THAT SHOULD HAVE ENDED IT

**Scene 6 — PORTRAIT — 2:10–2:55 (45s)**
*Visual: Illustrated match scene, muddy pitch, Vicenza vs Rimini, a challenge, Baggio going down and grabbing his knee.*

> Two days after Fiorentina's signing was confirmed, Baggio was still turning out for Vicenza. On May 5th, 1985, in a match against Rimini, he went down awkwardly and didn't get back up the same player.
> He'd torn the ACL and the meniscus in his right knee — a serious injury even by today's medicine, and in 1985, one that ended careers outright.
> Doctors weren't sure he'd ever play top-flight football again.

**Scene 7 — PORTRAIT — 2:55–3:40 (45s)**
*Visual: Illustrated hospital / recovery room, Baggio alone, a small Buddhist text on a bedside table, candlelight.*

> What happened during that recovery shaped the rest of his life, on and off the pitch.
> In pain, and facing months of uncertainty, Baggio moved away from the Catholic upbringing of his childhood and converted to Nichiren Buddhism, becoming a member of the Soka Gakkai International community.
> He would later say the practice gave him something medicine couldn't: a stillness he could return to, no matter what the scoreboard said. It's a detail that matters — because stillness is exactly what the world would remember him for, nine years later, for entirely the wrong reason.

**Scene 8 — DATA-GRAPHIC — 3:40–4:00 (20s)**
*Visual: Simple recovery timeline graphic — surgery, months of rehab, gradual return to fitness — ending on a Fiorentina badge.*

> Fiorentina backed him through the surgery and the long rehabilitation. It was a gamble on a player who might never fully recover.
> It paid off far beyond anything they could have predicted.

## CHAPTER 3 — IL DIVIN CODINO

**Scene 9 — PORTRAIT — 4:00–4:50 (50s)**
*Visual: Illustrated Stadio Artemio Franchi, purple shirts, Baggio scoring, growing his signature ponytail.*

> By the late 1980s, Baggio wasn't just back — he was Fiorentina's heartbeat. A close control that made defenders look like they were standing in wet cement. A ponytail that became as recognizable as his finishing.
> Florence gave him a nickname that would follow him for the rest of his career: **Il Divin Codino** — the Divine Ponytail.
> In a city that lives and breathes its football club, Baggio wasn't just a player. He belonged to Florence, and Florence believed he'd stay.

**Scene 10 — DATA-GRAPHIC — 4:50–5:10 (20s)**
*Visual: Simple stat card — goals and assists rising year over year at Fiorentina, late 1980s.*

> Season after season, the numbers climbed, and so did the expectation. By 1990, Roberto Baggio was one of the most talked-about players in Europe.
> Which made what happened that May all the more explosive.

## CHAPTER 4 — THE DIVORCE

**Scene 11 — DATA-GRAPHIC — 5:10–5:40 (30s)**
*Visual: Animated transfer graphic — Fiorentina badge to Juventus badge, a fee counter climbing past the previous world record (Ruud Gullit's move to AC Milan, £6 million) and settling around £8 million.*

> On May 18th, 1990, Fiorentina sold Roberto Baggio to Juventus for a fee of around £8 million — a new world record transfer, and to Fiorentina fans, a betrayal almost beyond words.
> Juventus wasn't just a rival. In Florence, Juventus was **the** rival — the club Fiorentina had clashed with for the Serie A title and in a bitter UEFA Cup final only a few years earlier.

**Scene 12 — PORTRAIT — 5:40–6:20 (40s)**
*Visual: Illustrated night scene, Florence streets, fire and smoke rendered in graphic-novel style, silhouettes of fans and police.*

> The city didn't take it quietly. The night after the transfer was announced, Florence descended into open unrest — barricades, fires, objects thrown at police. Roughly fifty people were injured and nine arrested.
> Baggio hadn't just changed clubs. In the eyes of his own city, he'd crossed a line that wasn't supposed to be crossable.

**Scene 13 — PORTRAIT — 6:20–7:00 (40s)**
*Visual: Illustrated Juventus vs Fiorentina match, Baggio awarded a penalty, then handing the ball to a teammate and walking away.*

> The following season, Juventus traveled to Florence to face Fiorentina, and the football gave Baggio one more twist. His new club won a penalty — one he was entitled to take.
> He refused it. He handed the ball to teammate Luigi De Agostini, who missed. Fiorentina won the match 1–0.
> Baggio never fully explained the gesture in those words, but everyone in that stadium understood it: whatever the transfer had done to his relationship with the city, he wasn't going to be the one to score the goal that rubbed it in.

## CHAPTER 5 — THE BEST PLAYER ON EARTH

**Scene 14 — DATA-GRAPHIC — 7:00–7:30 (30s)**
*Visual: Trophy graphic — UEFA Cup 1993, Ballon d'Or 1993, FIFA World Player of the Year 1993, all stacking onto a Juventus-era timeline.*

> Whatever Florence thought of the move, it worked, on the pitch, better than almost anyone could have imagined.
> At Juventus, Baggio won the UEFA Cup in 1993. That same year, he was named Ballon d'Or winner and FIFA World Player of the Year — the undisputed best footballer on the planet.
> He was twenty-six years old, and every door in world football was open to him. Then came the summer of 1994.

## CHAPTER 6 — CARRYING A NATION

**Scene 15 — DATA-GRAPHIC — 7:30–8:00 (30s)**
*Visual: Animated World Cup 1994 bracket, Italy's path highlighted — group stage struggles, then the knockout rounds.*

> Italy arrived at the 1994 World Cup in the United States looking shaky. They scraped through the group stage. In the Round of 16, against Nigeria, they were down 1–0 with time running out.
> Baggio scored the equalizer in the 88th minute, then won and converted a penalty in extra time to send Italy through. Without him, the tournament ends there.

**Scene 16 — PORTRAIT — 8:00–8:35 (35s)**
*Visual: Illustrated quarterfinal scene vs Spain — Baggio bursting past defenders in the dying minutes.*

> In the quarterfinal against Spain, Italy trailed again. Again, deep into the match, Baggio produced the moment that mattered — a driving run and finish that won the game.
> One player, carrying a team that, without him, wasn't going anywhere.

**Scene 17 — DATA-GRAPHIC — 8:35–9:05 (30s)**
*Visual: Semifinal stat card — Baggio's brace vs Bulgaria, 2-1 scoreline, Italy through to the final.*

> The semifinal against Bulgaria was, if anything, more emphatic: Baggio scored twice, and Italy won 2–1 to reach the World Cup Final.
> Four knockout matches. Nigeria, Spain, Bulgaria. Baggio was the difference in every single one. But he was carrying an injury of his own — a torn hamstring picked up in that Bulgaria match, and there was no time, and no real replacement, to let it heal.

**Scene 18 — PORTRAIT — 9:05–9:30 (25s)**
*Visual: Illustrated locker room, Baggio being strapped/taped, gritting through pain.*

> He played the final hurt. Nobody seriously suggested he shouldn't. He was the reason Italy was even there.

## CHAPTER 7 — THE MISS

**Scene 19 — DATA-GRAPHIC — 9:30–10:00 (30s)**
*Visual: Match graphic — 0-0 scoreline holding through 90, then extra time, both frozen at 0-0. First World Cup Final in history to be goalless after 120 minutes.*

> July 17th, 1994. The Rose Bowl, Los Angeles. Ninety minutes gone: nothing. Extra time: still nothing.
> For the first time in World Cup history, a final would be decided by a penalty shootout.

**Scene 20 — PORTRAIT — 10:00–10:40 (40s)**
*Visual: Illustrated shootout sequence — Baresi's miss, Massaro's miss, Brazil converting cleanly, building tension toward Baggio's turn.*

> It did not start well for Italy. Franco Baresi, their captain, missed. Daniele Massaro missed. Brazil, meanwhile, were clinical — only Márcio Santos would miss for them all night.
> By the time Roberto Baggio stepped up, Italy needed him to score just to keep the shootout alive. Brazil already led 3–2.

**Scene 21 — PORTRAIT — 10:40–11:25 (45s)**
*Visual: Slow-motion illustrated sequence — Baggio's run-up, goalkeeper Cláudio Taffarel diving, the ball rising over the bar.*

> Baggio had a plan: hit it down the middle, trusting that Taffarel, like most goalkeepers, would dive to a side. He was right about the goalkeeper. Taffarel did dive.
> But the shot itself rose too high. It cleared the crossbar by roughly three meters.
> Brazil had won their fourth World Cup. And the image that froze in the world's memory wasn't the celebration — it was Roberto Baggio, hands on hips, head down, standing completely still at the penalty spot while the stadium around him detonated.

**Scene 22 — PORTRAIT — 11:25–12:05 (40s)**
*Visual: Close illustrated portrait, unmoving, crowd blurred and celebrating in the background — deliberately still frame held longer than usual.*

> He didn't collapse. He didn't cover his face. He stood there, absorbing it, in front of ninety thousand people and a television audience of hundreds of millions.
> It's why the label stuck: **the man who died standing.** Not a metaphor for weakness — the opposite. He took the full weight of the worst moment of his career in front of the entire planet, without flinching, without hiding.
> "It is a wound that never closes," Baggio said, decades later. "To this day, I still haven't truly accepted that it happened. It haunts me."

## CHAPTER 8 — EXILE

**Scene 23 — DATA-GRAPHIC — 12:05–12:35 (30s)**
*Visual: Timeline graphic — Juventus to AC Milan 1995, Scudetto won 1996, but Baggio's minutes-played bar shown shrinking under a "Capello" label.*

> A shattered World Cup final didn't end Baggio's career, or even slow it down much. In 1995 he moved to AC Milan, and in his first season there, Milan won the Serie A title.
> But manager Fabio Capello never fully trusted Baggio in his system, and increasingly left him out or substituted him early — a public, prickly standoff between one of the most gifted players in the world and a manager determined not to build around him.
> Winning a title while barely playing is its own strange kind of exile.

**Scene 24 — PORTRAIT — 12:35–13:15 (40s)**
*Visual: Illustrated Bologna stadium, more modest, Baggio smiling, at ease, fully involved in play again.*

> In 1997, Baggio dropped down — in profile, not in ability — to Bologna, a club without Milan's or Juventus's ambitions. It turned out to be exactly what he needed.
> Free to play every week, in a system built around him, Baggio had one of the best individual seasons of his career, reminding everyone in Italy why he mattered — right as the next World Cup came into view.

## CHAPTER 9 — REDEMPTION WITHOUT REWARD

**Scene 25 — DATA-GRAPHIC — 13:15–13:40 (25s)**
*Visual: Squad graphic, Italy 1998, national team badge, "Cesare Maldini — Head Coach" label.*

> Bologna's form earned Baggio a recall to the Italy squad for the 1998 World Cup in France, under coach Cesare Maldini. But a recall wasn't the same as trust — Maldini used him sparingly, mostly from the bench.

**Scene 26 — PORTRAIT — 13:40–14:20 (40s)**
*Visual: Illustrated group-stage match vs Chile, Baggio stepping up to a penalty, ball into the corner, then walking away from his celebrating teammates without smiling.*

> Against Chile in the group stage, Baggio came on and scored a controlled penalty to level the match at 2–2. It should have been a moment of pure relief.
> Instead, as his teammates swarmed him, Baggio ducked away from the hugs, expression unreadable. The goal that mattered on the scoreboard clearly didn't settle whatever he was carrying underneath it.

**Scene 27 — DATA-GRAPHIC — 14:20–14:50 (30s)**
*Visual: Quarterfinal graphic, Italy vs France, July 3rd 1998, 0-0 after extra time, penalty shootout icons appearing one by one.*

> The quarterfinal against France went the same way as USA '94 — nothing after 120 minutes, another shootout, another chance for the moment to repeat itself in the worst way.
> This time, Roberto Baggio stepped up first.

**Scene 28 — PORTRAIT — 14:50–15:25 (35s)**
*Visual: Illustrated penalty, clean strike into the net, small controlled reaction from Baggio — then France's shootout continuing behind him.*

> He scored. Cleanly, confidently — the personal redemption arc football writers had been waiting four years to write.
> It didn't matter. Italy lost the shootout minutes later, eliminated from another World Cup on penalties, this time with Baggio having done everything right.
> Even his redemption came with no reward attached. That, as much as the miss itself, is the story of Roberto Baggio's relationship with penalty kicks — and with his own national team.

## CHAPTER 10 — THE LONG GOODBYE

**Scene 29 — DATA-GRAPHIC — 15:25–16:00 (35s)**
*Visual: Timeline graphic, Bologna → Inter Milan (1998) → Brescia (2000), with a separate line showing Italy caps simply stopping after 1998.*

> Baggio continued at the highest level for years afterward — Inter Milan, then Brescia — still scoring, still producing moments defenders couldn't plan for.
> But after the 1998 World Cup, he never played for the Italian national team again. Coach Giovanni Trapattoni left him out of the squads for Euro 2000 and the 2002 World Cup, decisions that remain debated by Italian football fans to this day.
> A player who had single-handedly carried his country to a World Cup final was simply no longer part of the plan.

**Scene 30 — PORTRAIT — 16:00–16:40 (40s)**
*Visual: Illustrated Brescia stadium, smaller, humbler, Baggio still playing with visible joy, older now, ponytail streaked grey.*

> At Brescia, in the twilight of his career, Baggio wasn't chasing titles anymore. He was simply playing — and playing well enough that even opposition fans began to treat watching him as a privilege they wouldn't have forever.

## CHAPTER 11 — FAREWELL

**Scene 31 — DATA-GRAPHIC — 16:40–17:05 (25s)**
*Visual: Simple date/venue card — May 16th, 2004, San Siro, Milan, Brescia away, final score Milan 4–2 Brescia.*

> On May 16th, 2004, Roberto Baggio played his final professional match — Brescia away at AC Milan, the club whose fans had once jeered him as a rival forward.
> Milan won 4–2. The scoreline barely mattered to anyone in the stadium that day.

**Scene 32 — PORTRAIT — 17:05–18:00 (55s)**
*Visual: Illustrated wide shot, San Siro at capacity — roughly 80,000 fans, Milan supporters included, all standing, applauding. Baggio being substituted, walking off in tears.*

> Six minutes from the end, Baggio was substituted. What happened next had nothing to do with club loyalty.
> All eighty thousand people inside San Siro stood and applauded — Brescia supporters, yes, but Milan's own fans too, the same fans who had spent a decade watching him torment their rivals. Chants of "Roberto! Roberto!" rolled around a stadium that, for one night, forgot which side it was supposed to be on.
> Baggio later called it "a precious memory full of gratitude" — one he said he'd carry with him for the rest of his life.
> It's worth sitting with that image next to the one from 1994. Both times, the whole stadium was on its feet. In LA, it was for Brazil, and Baggio stood alone in the middle of it. In Milan, ten years later, it was entirely for him.

## CHAPTER 12 — WHAT STANDING ACTUALLY MEANT

**Scene 33 — PORTRAIT — 18:00–18:50 (50s)**
*Visual: Illustrated montage — Baggio in Buddhist meditation, Baggio as FAO Goodwill Ambassador visiting a rural community, Baggio older, at peace.*

> After retiring, Baggio largely stepped away from the spotlight he'd never seemed entirely comfortable in. He became a Goodwill Ambassador for the UN's Food and Agriculture Organization, working on hunger relief — a long way, deliberately, from stadiums and shootouts.
> The Buddhist practice he'd picked up during that first devastating knee injury in 1985 stayed with him the whole way through — through the world records, the riots, the Ballon d'Or, the missed penalty, and the ovation that came a decade later.

**Scene 34 — PORTRAIT — 18:50–19:50 (60s)**
*Visual: Return to the opening image — the frozen 1994 penalty-spot portrait — but this time the camera slowly pulls back to reveal the 2004 San Siro ovation behind it, the two moments merging.*

> "The man who died standing" was never really about a missed penalty. It was about what he did in the sixty seconds after it — not running, not hiding, not blaming the goalkeeper or his manager or his hamstring. Just standing there, in front of the world, and absorbing the whole weight of it himself.
> Ten years later, an entire stadium — including the fans who'd once had every reason to hate him — stood right back up for him.
> That's the part of the story worth remembering. Not the miss. What came after it, on both nights.

*Visual: BY ATHAR bumper, subscribe/next-episode card.*

> This has been BY ATHAR.
> If this story moved you the way it moved us to tell it, subscribe — there are a lot more standing ovations, and a lot more penalty spots, still to come.
